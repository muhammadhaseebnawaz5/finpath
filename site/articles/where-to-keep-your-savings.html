Where to keep your savings: high-yield accounts, CDs and Treasury bills compared

Where you hold your cash is the single highest-return decision in personal finance that requires no risk-taking at all. The gap between the worst and the best cash account is often three or four percentage points — several hundred dollars a year on a modest balance — for identical safety.

FinPath Editorial Team ·10 min read

Key points

  • The difference between a legacy account paying 0.01% and a competitive one paying around 4% is roughly $400 a year on a $10,000 balance.
  • Cash options trade off three things only: access, rate and protection. There is no free lunch beyond that.
  • Keep emergency money in an insured instant-access account; use CDs or T-bills for money with a known date attached.
  • A CD ladder restores access without giving up the higher rate of a fixed term.
  • Interest is income. In the US it is taxed at your marginal rate; in the UK the personal savings allowance covers much of it — but the tax tail should not wag the rate decision.

Why the account matters more than the product

Most savings conversations are about what to invest in. The larger, easier and risk-free win usually comes first: money that is sitting in an account paying almost nothing while a competitor pays a normal rate for the same balance, the same protection and the same access.

A concrete example. $10,000 held for a year:

Where the money sitsIllustrative rateInterest in a year
Legacy branch account, untouched for years0.01%$1
Typical easy-access account3.00%$305
Competitive instant-access account4.30%$439
12-month fixed-term deposit4.60%$470

That is a $438 difference for one online transfer. Rates move constantly and differ by country, so treat the percentages as illustrative — the point is the shape of the gap, which has held in every interest-rate environment for the past decade.

The cheapest five minutes in personal finance

Check what rate your savings account currently pays, not what it paid when you opened it. Most banks quietly move long-standing accounts onto a lower "legacy" rate while advertising higher rates to new customers. Same bank, same protection, different price.

Notice what is not on the list: anything with an equity component, any "yield" product offering returns well above the central bank rate, and any account that requires you to lock money away for years to get a competitive rate. Those are investments or sales propositions wearing a savings label.

APY versus APR, and what compounding really does

Accounts quote one of two numbers, and confusing them costs money.

  • APY / AER (annual percentage yield / effective annual rate) includes compounding. This is the number to compare.
  • APR / nominal rate is the headline before compounding. A 4.30% APR compounded monthly is 4.38% APY.

On $10,000, monthly compounding instead of annual adds roughly $8 a year. Small — but it is the reason you compare accounts on APY, and the reason a "4.30% APR" account is not actually better than a "4.35% APY" one.

The bigger effect is time. $10,000 at 4% left alone for ten years with interest reinvested becomes about $14,800. Held in that 0.01% legacy account, it becomes about $10,010. The account choice, repeated every year, is worth more than most people realise — and it needs no market timing and no risk.

Switching accounts without breaking anything

The reason most people stay in a badly paying account is not laziness — it is the fear of getting something wrong mid-move. The sequence below avoids the two mistakes that actually happen: losing access for a week, and forgetting about direct debits.

  1. Open the new account first and fund it with a small amount. Verify on the regulator's checker that the institution is covered (FDIC, NCUA or FSCS). Never close the old account before the new one works.
  2. Move the bulk of the balance by bank transfer, keeping roughly one month of expenses in the old account for the rest of the month.
  3. Update anything that pays into the account — salary, pension, benefits, standing orders, direct debits. Make a written list before you start; this is the step where people get caught.
  4. Leave the old account open for one full billing cycle with a small balance. Any forgotten payment will surface, and a small balance covers it rather than bouncing.
  5. Close it once a clean month has passed — or keep it if it earns a useful rate or gives you a free buffer. There is no rule that says you must have one account.

One practical warning about "switching bonuses": they usually require a minimum deposit, a set number of transactions, or both, and they are taxable income in the US. Read the terms and check whether the bonus plus the ongoing rate actually beats a plain higher-rate account without the conditions.

The CD ladder: getting a fixed rate without losing access

The complaint about fixed-term deposits is real: the rate is better, but your money is stuck. A ladder solves it, and it takes about twenty minutes to set up.

Say you have $20,000 with no planned use in the next four years. Instead of one four-year deposit, split it:

PortionTermMatures inWhat happens then
$5,0001 year12 monthsReinvest for 4 years, or spend if plans changed
$5,0002 years24 monthsReinvest for 4 years
$5,0003 years36 monthsReinvest for 4 years
$5,0004 years48 monthsReinvest for 4 years

After the first year, a portion matures every twelve months, so you always have a decision point and access within a year, while the bulk of the money is earning fixed-term rates. Two practical notes: keep the emergency fund out of the ladder (it needs to be instant), and check the early-withdrawal terms before you commit — some accounts allow withdrawal after a penalty of a few months' interest, others do not allow it at all.

How the interest is taxed

Interest is income in both countries, but the treatment differs enough to change the maths.

United StatesUnited Kingdom
How interest is taxedAt your marginal federal rate, plus state tax where applicableAt your marginal rate, after the personal savings allowance
AllowanceNone — bank interest is taxable from the first dollar for most filers£1,000 for basic-rate taxpayers, £500 for higher-rate, £0 for additional-rate
ReportingBank issues a 1099-INT; you report it on your returnUsually taken via PAYE coding or self-assessment
Tax-free wrapperNone for cash specifically; Roth and IRA growth is sheltered but withdrawals are restrictedCash ISA — interest is tax-free and the allowance renews each tax year

Two consequences worth internalising. First, in the US a 4.5% account is really about 3.6% after federal tax at 22% — still better than 0.01%, so the account switch remains the right call. Second, in the UK, if your interest is going to exceed the allowance, a cash ISA at a slightly lower headline rate can beat a taxable account at a higher one. Run both numbers.

Seven traps in cash savings

  1. The teaser rate. A high rate for three or six months, then a quiet drop. Diarise the end date and move the money.
  2. Notice accounts. Better rates that need 30–90 days' notice. Fine for planned money, useless for an emergency fund.
  3. Monthly fees. An account charging $10 a month needs a very large balance to justify itself against a free one.
  4. Withdrawal limits. Some accounts restrict the number of withdrawals per year and cut your rate if you exceed them.
  5. Fixed-term deposits with no stated early-exit route. Check the penalty mechanics before committing, not after.
  6. "Savings" products that are investments. Structured products, bond funds and investment-linked plans can lose money. Read what the money is actually held in.
  7. Anything promising returns far above the central bank rate. That premium is being paid for a risk you have not been told about. In the US, check the SEC's investor alerts; in the UK, the FCA warning list.

Compare accounts in ten minutes

  • APY / AER today — and how long the rate is guaranteed for
  • Is it a deposit with an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union) / FSCS-covered firm? Verify on the regulator's own checker, not the bank's page
  • Access: instant, same-day, or fixed term?
  • Minimum balance for the advertised rate
  • Fees: monthly, paper statement, early withdrawal, over-withdrawal
  • Tax treatment, and whether a tax-free wrapper available in your country beats it
  • Whether the rate applies to the whole balance or just a tier of it
Order of operations

1) Keep one to two months of expenses in an instant-access account at your everyday bank so transfers are immediate. 2) Move the rest of the emergency fund to a competitive insured account, ideally at a different institution. 3) Once the fund is full, build a ladder for money with a date attached — a car replacement, a tax bill, a deposit. The emergency-fund guide explains how to work out the target; this page is about where the target should sit.

Questions people actually ask

Is my money safe if the bank fails?

Deposits with FDIC-insured banks (US) are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution; NCUA covers credit unions at the same limit. UK deposits with FSCS-covered firms are protected up to £85,000 per person, per firm. Coverage is per institution, so splitting a large balance between two banks can increase what is protected — but check the regulator's official ownership rules, because some brands share a single licence.

Should I chase the highest rate in the market?

Chasing an extra 0.15% at an unfamiliar institution is rarely worth the admin. Comparing your current legacy rate with a competitive mainstream one is where the real money is.

Are online-only banks riskier?

Not if they hold the same deposit-insurance coverage — the protection attaches to the licence, not the branch network. Verify the licence on the regulator's checker before depositing, and keep in mind that service and transfer speeds vary.

Should I put my emergency fund in a CD?

Only the portion you are sure you will not need. An emergency fund exists to be available on a bad Tuesday; breaking a fixed term to access it defeats the purpose.

How often should I check the rate?

Every three to six months, and whenever central bank rates move. A five-minute check twice a year captures most of the benefit.
